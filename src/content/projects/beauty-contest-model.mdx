Not all spectrum is auctioned. In a comparative selection process, sometimes called a beauty
contest, the regulator scores proposals against published criteria and awards to the best one.
Coverage obligations, deployment timetables, wholesale access terms, sometimes price.

This is harder to prepare for than an auction, and the reason is worth stating plainly: in an
auction you are trying to work out what the spectrum is worth to you. In a beauty contest you
are trying to work out what a *promise* costs you, and then how many points that promise buys.

## Costing a commitment

Every criterion in a beauty contest translates into a commitment, and every commitment has a
cost that lands somewhere on a fifteen-year cash flow.

A coverage obligation is a rollout schedule: sites, backhaul, power, civils, and the operating
cost of running them in places that will not pay for themselves. A deployment deadline is an
option you have sold, because it removes your ability to wait for equipment prices to fall. A
wholesale access commitment is margin given away on traffic you would otherwise have carried
yourself.

Each of those is modelled as an incremental cash flow and discounted at WACC. That gives a cost
per commitment in present value terms, which is the only basis on which two criteria can
sensibly be compared.

## Where the uncertainty goes

Point estimates are worse than useless here because the inputs genuinely are not known. Build
cost per site, subscriber uptake in low-density areas, and equipment price trajectories all have
real distributions rather than values.

So the commitment costs run as a Monte Carlo, with the correlations that matter left in.
Rollout cost and uptake are not independent, and treating them as if they were understates the
variance in exactly the direction that makes an aggressive bid look safe.

The output is a distribution of the total cost of a proposal, which supports a different and
better question than "what does this cost". It supports "what is the chance this proposal costs
more than the spectrum is worth to us".

## Scoring the scoring rule

The part that surprised me most. The published scoring rule is itself a model input, and it is
usually more sensitive than it looks.

Most schemes normalise each criterion against the best submission, then apply weights. That
means your score depends on what everyone else offered, so the marginal value of one more
percentage point of coverage is a function of competitor behaviour you cannot observe. Small
changes in weighting, or in how a criterion is normalised, can reorder the outcome entirely.

The model therefore treats the scoring rule as something to run sensitivities across rather than
a fixed constraint. The useful output is the region where a proposal wins across most plausible
competitor scenarios, not the single proposal that maximises the score against one guess.

## What it produced

A defensible answer to the question that actually gets asked in the room: how much more can we
commit before the award stops being worth winning. That is a number, it has a distribution
around it, and both come from the same model.
