Buy asset packs for a few years and you end up with a problem that is genuinely annoying: tens
of thousands of models, textures, sounds and animations across a dozen libraries, in folder
structures nobody designed to be browsed together.

The practical symptom is that you cannot find what you own. So you hand-roll something you
already have, or worse, buy it a second time.

## What it does

Indexes every pack into one searchable catalogue, with a preview for everything.

Meshes get a rendered thumbnail plus triangle count, bounds, material slots and whether they
carry a rig. Textures get a thumbnail, resolution and channel layout, which is the bit that
actually decides whether a texture is usable in a given pipeline. Audio gets a waveform, a
duration and an inline player. Animations get frame count, duration and the rig they target,
because an animation on the wrong rig is worse than no animation.

Search runs across name, pack, type, tags and the extracted metadata, so "rigged character
under 20k triangles" is a query rather than an afternoon.

## Building it

Thumbnail generation is the whole job. Blender runs headless in batch to render mesh previews,
ffmpeg handles audio waveforms and video, and image formats are processed directly. It is
embarrassingly parallel and entirely IO-bound, so the work went into a job queue with a
concurrency cap and resumability, because a full index of a large library takes hours and must
survive being interrupted.

Everything is content-hashed. Re-indexing only touches what actually changed, and identical
files that appear in three packs under three names are collapsed into one entry with three
paths. That last part removed more duplication than I expected.

The catalogue is SQLite with full-text search over the metadata. Local, single file, no server,
fast enough at this scale that anything more would be architecture for its own sake.

## Licensing

The viewer indexes and previews assets already licensed to me. It stores paths and derived
metadata rather than redistributing anything, and it is a local tool, not a service. That
distinction is deliberate: most of these packs permit use in projects but not redistribution,
and a tool that quietly blurred that line would be a liability rather than a convenience.

## Why it is worth having

The real return is not organisation, it is scope. Knowing what is already available changes
what feels feasible at the start of a project, and the answer to "can we do X" is much more
often yes when you can see that most of X is already sitting on the drive.
