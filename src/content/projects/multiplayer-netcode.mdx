Three multiplayer projects with genuinely different constraints, and the thing they taught me is
that netcode architecture is decided by one question: what happens when two players disagree.

## Authority

Every design here is host-authoritative, and the reason is simple. If clients are authoritative
over their own state, the game is only as honest as its least honest player, and the failure is
not detectable from inside the system.

So the host simulates and everyone else predicts. Inputs go up, authoritative state comes back,
and clients reconcile the difference. The cost is latency on your own actions, which is the
problem prediction exists to hide.

## Prediction and reconciliation

Clients apply input locally the moment it happens, then keep a buffer of unacknowledged inputs.
When authoritative state arrives, the client rewinds to it and replays the buffer forward.

Most of the time the replay lands where the prediction already was and nothing visible happens.
When it does not, you get a correction, and the entire craft is in making corrections
unnoticeable. Snapping is honest and looks terrible; smoothing looks better and lies about
position. The compromise is to snap anything the player cannot see and smooth what they can.

In [Padel](/projects/padel) the hard case is a ball off the back glass, where wall contact and
the reconciliation window land in the same frame. Correcting the ball after a bounce feels
wrong in a way correcting a player never does, because the bounce is the thing the player was
reading.

## Bandwidth is the constraint, not CPU

The instinct is to send more, more often. The discipline is to send less.

Delta compression against the last acknowledged state, quantised positions and rotations rather
than full floats, interest management so a client is only told about what it could plausibly
perceive, and an update rate well below frame rate with interpolation covering the gap.

For [Cat Dog Fish](/projects/cat-dog-fish) the constraint inverted: the payloads are tiny but
correctness is everything, so the server sends full state snapshots and simply refuses to
include other players' in-progress answers. Cheap, and it makes cheating structurally impossible
rather than merely difficult.

## Data structures that earn their place

**Spatial partitioning** for the horror game's AI. Naive "which enemies can see the player"
is O(n) per enemy per frame against every occluder. A uniform grid over the level turns the
broad phase into a handful of cell lookups, and the expensive raycast only runs on what
survives it.

**Ring buffers** for input and state history. Fixed size, no allocation, and the reconciliation
path runs every frame on the client, so an allocation there is a stutter you can feel.

**A\* with a navmesh** for pathfinding, with the usual caveat that the heuristic matters more
than the implementation. An inadmissible heuristic is faster and produces paths that look
subtly wrong, and "looks wrong" is the only metric that counts in a game.

**Hash-based deduplication** for the word game's scoring, which is O(n) per category rather
than the O(n²) pairwise comparison the problem naively suggests. With ten players and sixteen
categories that difference does not matter at all, which is worth saying: I picked it because
it is simpler to read, not because it is faster.

## The general lesson

Most of the performance work that mattered was not optimisation. It was choosing a structure
whose worst case was acceptable, then not having to think about it again. The profiler is for
finding out you were wrong about that, not for shaving constants off code that should not have
been in the hot path.
