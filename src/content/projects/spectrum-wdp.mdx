Combinatorial auctions let bidders bid on packages rather than individual lots, which is the
right shape for spectrum because a block is worth more next to another block you already hold.
The cost is that deciding who won stops being obvious.

## Winner determination

The winner determination problem is: given a set of package bids, choose the subset that
maximises total value while never selling the same lot twice. It is equivalent to weighted set
packing and is NP-hard in general, which Rothkopf, Pekeč and Harstad set out along with the
structures that stay tractable.[^rothkopf] Sandholm's treatment is the one I worked from for
the search formulation and the pruning that makes realistic instances solvable.[^sandholm]

In practice spectrum auctions are far from the worst case. Lots are few, packages are
structured, and bidders are subject to eligibility and activity rules that prune the space
before the solver ever sees it. I formulate it as a mixed integer program and solve directly;
the interesting work is in generating the instances, not in beating the solver.

## Why pricing is the harder half

Getting the allocation right is the easy part. What the winners should pay is where the design
actually lives.

Vickrey-Clarke-Groves is truthful and efficient, and almost never used. Ausubel and Milgrom's
account of why is the clearest statement of the problem: VCG can produce revenues below the
core, so a losing coalition would have been willing to pay more than the winners did, which is
politically indefensible when the seller is a government selling public spectrum.[^lovely]

The answer the literature converged on is core-selecting payments: charge the minimum that
leaves no coalition able to profitably object. Day and Milgrom set out the core-selecting family
and its incentive properties;[^daymilgrom] Day and Raghavan gave the constraint generation
approach that makes the bidder-optimal core point actually computable rather than merely
definable.[^dayraghavan]

The lab implements VCG, the bidder-optimal core, and a nearest-VCG core rule, so the same
instance can be run through all three and the revenue gap inspected directly. That gap is the
number that matters in a real design conversation, and it is much easier to argue about when
you can show it.

## Validating against the literature

The part I insisted on. An auction solver that produces plausible numbers is worthless, because
plausible is exactly what a subtly wrong implementation produces.

So the algorithms are checked by recreating published experimental conditions and confirming the
implementation reproduces the reported results before it is pointed at anything real. Cramton's
survey of spectrum auction design supplied the practical grounding on what real regulators
actually run and where theory and practice diverge.[^cramton] Milgrom's account of the
simultaneous ascending auction covers the format most of the European awards I work on actually
use.[^milgrom]

Where a paper's data is available I run it. Where it is not, I recreate the generator from the
description and check the aggregate behaviour matches.

## Status

A working lab rather than a product: the solver, the three payment rules, an instance generator
and the validation harness. It exists to answer questions about auction design with a number
instead of an intuition.
