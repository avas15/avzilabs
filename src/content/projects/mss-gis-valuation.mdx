Terrestrial spectrum benchmarking usually runs on national aggregates: price per MHz per head of
population, adjusted for licence term. It is crude but it works, because a national mobile
network really does aim to cover most of a country.

Mobile satellite spectrum breaks that assumption completely, and the standard method quietly
gives the wrong answer.

## Why population per head fails here

A satellite band's value is not distributed like a country's population. Its advantage is
precisely in the places terrestrial networks do not reach: at sea, over mountains, across the
areas where putting up a mast is uneconomic or impossible.

Valuing it on national population credits it for urban users who will never use it, and gives it
no credit for the maritime and remote coverage that is the entire commercial case. The
directional error is large and consistent.

## Doing it geospatially instead

The tooling works on a grid rather than a country.

Gridded population data is intersected with terrain and with modelled terrestrial coverage to
classify every cell: well served, marginally served, or genuinely uncovered. Maritime zones are
brought in separately, since the addressable demand there is vessels and routes rather than
residents, and needs its own density model.

The result is an addressable population and an addressable area that mean something for this
band specifically, instead of a national figure that describes a different product.

## Feeding it into value

From there the valuation is conventional and deliberately so: addressable demand drives a
subscriber and ARPU model, that drives cash flows, those discount at WACC, and the whole thing
runs as a Monte Carlo because the uptake assumptions in remote and maritime segments are wide.

The geospatial work is upstream of the finance, not a substitute for it. What changes is the
input quality, and that is where the error was.

## The comparison that made the case

The clearest output was running both methods on the same band and showing the gap. Where a
naive per-head benchmark and a coverage-weighted model disagree by a large multiple, the
argument stops being about methodology preference and becomes about which number to put in
front of a board.

## Practical notes

Most of the effort was not modelling. It was projections, resolution mismatches between
population rasters and coverage polygons, and coastline handling, where a small definitional
choice about how far offshore to count moves the maritime figure substantially. That definition
is an input to the model, stated explicitly, rather than an accident of the data.

Method only. The datasets, bands and commercial inputs are not published.
